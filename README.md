## What makes a KVM VPS different from a cheap container VPS?

KVM stands for Kernel-based Virtual Machine and provides hardware-assisted virtualization rather than simply placing multiple containers on a shared host kernel.

That matters for users who want more control over the virtual machine. A genuine KVM VPS can normally run its own Linux kernel and is much closer to a standalone virtual machine from an operating-system perspective.

That distinction comes up repeatedly in current VPS comparisons. Several 2026 guides explicitly filter for KVM rather than OpenVZ or LXC when comparing budget VPS products, because container-based virtualization can impose different limitations on kernels, isolation and workloads.

For a simple website, the difference might never be obvious. For Docker, custom kernel features, certain networking setups, virtualization-related software, or more demanding self-hosting, it can become much more relevant.

The other side of the equation is resources. KVM does not magically make a 1 GB VPS powerful. A KVM server with 1 vCPU and 1 GB RAM is still a very small server.

That is why comparing only the word “KVM” is just another version of comparing only the price.

## Monthly versus annual billing: where the real comparison begins

Budget VPS buyers often make one of two mistakes.

The first is choosing the lowest monthly figure without checking what it buys.

The second is seeing a low annual price and assuming it is automatically better.

An annual plan changes the risk calculation. Paying ¥199 once is cheaper than paying ¥68 every month, but you are committing the money up front and accepting the specific configuration for that term.

The annual offers also do not necessarily correspond to the provider's standard monthly tiers. LisaHost's annual specials can have smaller storage, lower transfer allowances or different network characteristics from the normal monthly plans.

## Which specifications matter most for a cheap KVM VPS?

### RAM is usually the first hard limit

A 512 MB VPS is suitable for very small workloads.

A 1 GB server gives you more breathing room, but it is still an entry-level machine.

Once you run a web server, database, Docker containers and background processes together, RAM becomes much more important.

That is why Hostinger's current KVM 1 looks quite different from some sub-$3 VPS offers: it includes 4 GB RAM.

For a static site or tiny utility, 1 GB can be completely reasonable.

For several applications, 2–4 GB is a much more comfortable baseline.

### Storage is more than a number

Ten gigabytes can sound adequate until you install an operating system, logs, Docker images, databases, backups and application files.

LisaHost's entry-level annual plans often use 10 GB storage, while higher monthly tiers move to 20 GB, 40 GB or 80 GB.

For a disposable test server, 10 GB is fine.

For a server you expect to keep for years, storage growth should be part of the buying decision.

### Bandwidth and traffic quotas are not interchangeable

A provider can advertise a high port speed while giving you a relatively small monthly transfer allowance.

LisaHost's ¥68 New York plan, for example, lists a 300 Mbps connection and 3 TB monthly traffic. Its unlimited-traffic Lite plan is a different product at ¥198/month and lowers the port speed to 200 Mbps while removing the monthly traffic cap.

That is a good example of why “unlimited bandwidth” can be a misleading phrase.

Port speed tells you the maximum connection capacity.

Traffic tells you how much data you can actually transfer under the plan.

They answer different questions.

### IPv4 is still worth checking

An included IPv4 address is significant for many ordinary VPS workloads.

LisaHost's current entry-level products commonly include one IPv4 address. RackNerd also explicitly includes one free IP on its listed KVM plans. DigitalOcean's pricing is resource-based, so networking and IP requirements should be checked separately when building a real cost estimate.

This is one of those small details that can turn a headline $4 VPS into a different monthly total once add-ons are included.

If RAM is consistently full, add RAM.

If CPU is saturated, add CPU.

If storage is filling, add disk.

If traffic is the constraint, buy a plan whose transfer allowance matches the workload.
